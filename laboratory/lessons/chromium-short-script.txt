This beaker contains a sample of a process stream from a chemical processing plant. It may look innocent, but it can be deadly. It contains high levels of hexavalent chromium.

We need to reduce toxic chromium six to less toxic chromium three. We need a reducing agent: a compound we can snatch electrons from.

A Pourbaix diagram provides our redox map. Unlike chromium six, chromium three forms a highly insoluble hydroxide solid.

Add iron two sulfate as a reducing agent. Iron two becomes iron three. Chromium six accepts those electrons and becomes chromium three. Watch the arrow.

Add sodium hydroxide until chromium hydroxide precipitation reaches its maximum in this model. Watch the pH move across the map.

Replay with solubility curves. Chromium three is highlighted. Iron three and excess iron two are shown too.

Let the solids settle, then filter and collect the mixed metal precipitates. Congratulations: a successful separation! Almost all the chromium is now out of the process water.

Alternatively, keep the iron and chromium solutions in separate beakers. Platinum electrodes and a salt bridge let electrons travel through a wire.

Iron two is oxidized at the anode. Chromium six is reduced at the cathode. This illustrates the redox route; the chromium still needs precipitation and collection.
