This beaker contains a sample of a process stream from a chemical processing plant. It may look innocent, but it can be deadly. It contains high levels of hexavalent chromium.

The wastewater cannot leave the plant like this. First, change the oxidation state. Then separate the chromium from the water.

A Pourbaix diagram provides our redox map of the system. We want chromium three, which we can precipitate as a hydroxide.

Add iron two sulfate. Iron two becomes iron three. Chromium six accepts those electrons and becomes chromium three. Watch the arrow.

Add sodium hydroxide and raise the pH. Green chromium hydroxide and rusty iron hydroxide precipitate together. Filter out these mixed solids.

Calcium remains in the filtrate. When it meets carbonate-rich water downstream, it could form scale and block valves. Let us collect it here.

Baking soda supplies carbonate. With the pH adjusted, white calcium carbonate precipitates. Filter again, and verify the treated water before discharge.

Alternatively, keep the iron and chromium solutions in separate beakers. Platinum electrodes and a salt bridge let electrons travel through a wire.

Iron two is oxidized at the anode. Chromium six is reduced at the cathode. This illustrates the redox route; the chromium still needs precipitation and collection.
