In front of you is a solution of silver nitrate. It contains thirty grams of silver. You cannot see it: the silver is dissolved as ions. That is quite a lot of money. So, how do we get it back?

Table salt provides chloride. Add it, and the equilibrium shifts towards precipitation. Silver chloride forms a solid. The silver was here all along.

We would prefer pure metallic silver, not a silver salt precipitate. Silver metal. Oxidation state zero.

Back to our original solution. A Pourbaix diagram provides our redox map of the system.

Silver ions above this line. Silver metal below. But simply assigning a reduction potential on a diagram does not make a reaction happen in real life. We need a reagent that supplies electrons to bring about a redox reaction.

Copper’s boundary lies lower. Copper reacts spontaneously with silver ions. Add copper powder: silver ions snatch electrons from copper and become metallic silver, forming fine dust and chunks.

A spontaneous reaction has free energy available to do electrical work. Instead of mixing everything, separate the reactions. Send the electrons through a wire.

Take a fresh silver solution. Beside it, copper ions and a copper electrode. Connect a salt bridge and wire. Silver ions take electrons from copper and plate onto the other electrode.

If you already have a chunk of silver lying around, make it bigger by plating the silver in our solution onto it. Same reaction, now working for you.
