In front of you is a solution of silver nitrate. It contains thirty grams of silver. You cannot see it: the silver is dissolved as ions. That is quite a lot of money. So, how do we get it back?

Table salt provides chloride. Add it, and the equilibrium shifts right. Silver chloride forms a solid. The silver was here all along.

But silver chloride is not something you take to the pawn shop. We want silver metal. Oxidation state zero.

Back to our original solution. This Pourbaix diagram shows pH across and potential up. Higher means more oxidizing.

Silver ions above this line. Silver metal below. But a dot supplies no electrons. We need a donor.

Copper’s boundary lies lower. Copper reacts spontaneously with silver ions. Add copper powder: copper dissolves, leaving fine silver dust and chunks.

A spontaneous reaction has free energy available to do electrical work. Instead of mixing everything, separate the reactions. Send the electrons through a wire.

Take a fresh silver solution. Beside it, copper ions and a copper electrode. Connect a salt bridge and wire. Copper dissolves. Silver plates onto the other electrode.

If you already have a chunk of silver lying around, make it bigger by plating the silver in our solution onto it. Same reaction, now working for you.
